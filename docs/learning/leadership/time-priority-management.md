## Description

Build season is a fixed clock and an infinite list. Volunteer hours are
not. The leader who stays "busy" until midnight is not automatically the
one who ships the drivetrain. The leader who protects the **important**
work — testing, documentation, training the next operator — is the one
the team still has in Week 6. Titles do not create time. Influence is
how you say "not that, not tonight" and have people believe you.

The **Eisenhower matrix** sorts work on two axes:

- **Important and urgent** — do now (a loose wheel the night before a
  week-zero scrimmage).
- **Important, not urgent** — schedule it (driver practice, wiring
  labels, the successor who has never run standup). Teams skip this
  quadrant and then panic.
- **Urgent, not important** — minimize or delegate (most "got a
  second?" interruptions, decorative stickers before the mechanism
  works).
- **Neither** — drop it.

Picture a Thursday night with fifteen people in the shop and no named
gate. Two students are reprinting a decorative logo. A lead is in a
forty-minute design argument about a feature that is not on the
critical path. The person who could tap the gearbox plates is answering
"got a second?" questions at the tool crib. The plates stay raw. Friday
the programmer cannot test. That is not a work-ethic problem. That is a
priority problem, and a title will not fix it — only a person with
enough influence to point at the gate and send the logo back to the
parking lot.

Ticket 5's standup goes bad when every item is treated as important and
urgent. Ticket 6's delegation is how the urgent-not-important work
leaves your hands. Ticket 7's expectations are how you tell stakeholders
that a neither item will not happen. Ticket 8's conflicts often start
when two people both think their fire is the only fire.

This ticket is triage for *your* group: rank by impact, find the
critical path, limit work in progress, and protect a block of focus.
Servant leadership here looks unglamorous. You unblock someone for
twenty minutes so they can use the mill. You stop a third parallel
redesign so the first one can get finished. You go home on time once so
the team does not learn that martyrdom is the culture (ticket 11 will
come back to that).

**Critical path** is the chain that actually gates the rest of the
robot (or the event). If the gearbox plates are not tapped, assembly
cannot start, wiring cannot start, software cannot test. Everything
else is a side quest until that chain moves. **Work in progress** is
how many of those quests you have started. A small volunteer group
doing three things to "done" will beat ten things at 40 percent every
time — because 40 percent does not inspect.

This site does not track whether you finished. The matrix, the critical
path, and the focus-time experiment live in your Leadership notes.

## What you'll learn

- How to sort a live task list into the four Eisenhower quadrants.
- How to find the critical path on a small subsystem and protect it.
- Why limiting work in progress beats starting another "quick" idea.
- How to protect one block of focus time this week, for you or the
  group.

## Tasks

1. **Read a short priority primer.** Read
   [The Eisenhower Matrix](https://www.eisenhower.me/eisenhower-matrix/)
   and skim David Allen's *Getting Things Done* idea of a trusted next
   action if you have a copy — you do not need the whole GTD system.
   Optional: your team's event calendar so "urgent" is a real date, not
   a feeling.

2. **Sort a real list.** Take every open item from your RACI, the last
   used standup agenda, and anything living in your head. Put at least
   twelve items into the four quadrants in a dated `Ticket 9 — Time`
   section. For each item, one line: why that quadrant, not just the
   label. Star the important-not-urgent items you have been skipping
   (training, documentation, a backup person on the RACI's A).

3. **Find the critical path.** For the current robot (or outreach)
   milestone, write the chain of tasks that actually gates the rest.
   Example: "plates lasered → tapped → assembled → wired → software
   can test." Circle the current gate. Write who is Accountable for
   that gate (ticket 3) and what you will do in the next 48 hours to
   unblock it. If you cannot name the gate, you do not have a plan —
   you have a pile.

4. **Limit work in progress.** Count how many things your group has
   started and not finished. Pick a WIP limit the group can defend
   (often two or three for a handful of people). In the notes, name
   what you will **pause** so the critical path can move. Say that
   pause out loud at the next standup. Parking-lot it on the agenda
   you already know how to run.

5. **Protect one focus block.** Schedule a 45–90 minute block this
   week where the group (or you) will not take "quick questions." Put
   it on a calendar or a whiteboard. Tell people when you *are*
   interruptible. Afterward, write what you finished that would not
   have survived a night of context switching. Batch the leftover
   questions.

6. **Personal top three and mentor pass.** Before the next work
   session, write the day's top one to three priorities *before* you
   pick up a tool. Afterward, mark what actually happened. Show a
   mentor the matrix, the critical path, and the WIP pause. Ask them
   where the team is addicted to urgent-not-important work. Add their
   answer.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Notes include at least twelve live items sorted into Eisenhower
      quadrants with a one-line why for each.
- [ ] You wrote a critical path for a current milestone, named the
      current gate, and named the Accountable person.
- [ ] You set a WIP limit, named something you paused, and said it at
      a real standup or work session.
- [ ] You scheduled and used one focus block and wrote what it
      protected.
- [ ] You planned one session with a written top one-to-three and
      compared it to what happened.
- [ ] A mentor reviewed the matrix and the critical path. Work stays
      in your Leadership notes, not on this website.

## Notes

- Important-not-urgent is where succession (ticket 12) and culture
  (ticket 11) live. If this matrix is all fires, you are borrowing
  from next year's team.
- Clearing a blocker for someone else can be the highest-leverage hour
  you spend. That is still leadership work, not "not real work."
- A fully packed personal plan breaks at the first broken tap. Leave
  slack on purpose.
- Do not use "priority" as a way to ignore people. Ticket 2 still
  applies: if you cannot help now, say when you can.
- The next ticket, [Decision-Making & Problem-Solving](../decision-making/),
  is how you choose *which* important thing wins when two gates both
  look real and the clock is running.
