## Description

On a volunteer FRC team, almost every ugly week is a communication week in
disguise. The intake owner thought "soon" meant Thursday. The programmer
heard "we'll integrate Friday" as "the mechanism exists." A freshman asked a
question in chat, got a sarcastic reply, and stopped asking. Nobody is paid
to absorb that. They just drift.

Ticket 1 asked you to earn influence by serving and by example. Influence
dies in the first conversation where you do not listen, or where you are
kind-but-vague, or where you drop a hard message in a 40-message Slack
thread. This ticket is the daily skill the rest of the track assumes:
**listen so the other person feels heard, then speak so they know what to
do.**

*Crucial Conversations* (Patterson, Grenny, McMillan, and Switzler) is built
for talks where stakes are high, opinions differ, and emotions are hot — a
design argument at 9 p.m., a missed commitment before a district event, a
parent waiting in the parking lot. Two ideas from that book are enough for
this ticket. **Start with Heart:** before you open your mouth, ask what you
really want for you, for them, and for the relationship — not "win" or
"punish" or "get this over with." **Pool of shared meaning:** each person
walks in with their own facts and stories; good dialogue puts more of that
into the open so the group can decide with a fuller picture. You will
practice those ideas on ordinary shop talk first. High-stakes conflict is
ticket 8.

Clear and kind is not a contradiction. "Can you finish the intake bracket
by Thursday 7 p.m., holes matching the current CAD, and ping me if you are
blocked?" is kinder than "we should work on the intake" followed by silent
disappointment. Kind is not soft. Avoiding the sentence is not kindness.

Channel matters on a small team. Chat is for coordination. A wall of text
is a bad place for "I felt dismissed when you talked over me." Public praise,
private correction — that habit will show up again when you practice
feedback in ticket 4.

This site does not track whether you finished. Keep the work in the
Leadership notes you started in
[What Leadership Really Is](../what-leadership-is/). If your team exported
these tickets, close the issue there after a mentor accepts the criteria.

## Prerequisites

- [What Leadership Really Is](../what-leadership-is/) completed (Leadership
  notes started, mentor conversation done).

## What you'll learn

- How to listen actively: attention, no interrupting, reflect back, open
  questions.
- How to write a request that is specific about owner, outcome, and time.
- How to pick a channel that matches the emotional and informational load.
- How *Crucial Conversations* "Start with Heart" and "pool of shared
  meaning" apply to ordinary FRC coordination, not only blow-ups.

## Tasks

1. **Read the communication ideas.** Read
   [Crucial Conversations skill summary: Start with Heart](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-start-with-heart/)
   and skim the
   [Crucial Learning glossary](https://cruciallearning.com/glossary/)
   entries for *Crucial Conversation*, *Start with Heart*, and *Pool of
   Shared Meaning*. Then read MindTools'
   [Active Listening](https://www.mindtools.com/az4wxv7/active-listening)
   (or the same article via a library/school login if the site gates it).
   If a mentor has the *Crucial Conversations* book, read the "Start with
   Heart" chapter instead of the summary. You do not need the whole book
   yet.

2. **Write the ticket 2 reading notes.** In your Leadership notes, under a
   dated `Ticket 2 — Communication` heading, write:

   - two sentences: what "Start with Heart" means in a shop argument
   - two sentences: what a "pool of shared meaning" would have changed in
     a recent mix-up on *your* team (wrong deadline, wrong CAD, wrong
     who-owns-it)
   - the four active-listening moves in your own words: full attention,
     let them finish, reflect back, open question

3. **Rewrite three vague messages.** Copy these into the notes and rewrite
   each so a volunteer who is also in three AP classes knows the owner,
   the outcome, the "done" picture, and the time. Add a line naming the
   **channel** you would use (in person, voice, chat, email) and why.

   - "We should really get electrical done."
   - "Can someone look at the intake sometime?"
   - A message you actually sent or received this season that was fuzzy.
     If you cannot find one, use: "The bumper plan is kind of a mess,
     please handle it."

   Kind and clear. No sarcasm. No "as I said yesterday."

4. **Practice active listening in a real conversation.** This is not a
   role-play if you can avoid it. At the next work session, pick a
   teammate who is explaining a problem (a jammed roller, a failed deploy,
   a sponsor email they are stuck on). For at least three minutes:

   - phone down, face them
   - do not jump to a fix
   - reflect back: "So what I'm hearing is…"
   - ask one open question: "What would help?" or "What have you already
     tried?"

   Then, and only then, offer a suggestion if they still want one. Write
   a short debrief in the notes: what they said, what you reflected, the
   question you asked, and whether you interrupted. If the only option is
   a mentor or a parent, that still counts — the skill is the same.

5. **Close the loop once.** After any real coordination conversation this
   week (chat or in person), end it by confirming **who** does **what** by
   **when**. Write the sentence you used in the notes. Example: "I'll
   update the hole pattern tonight; you'll dry-fit Friday; we check
   together at 5:30." Communication is not done when you have spoken. It
   is done when both people can repeat the next step.

6. **Mentor check.** Show a mentor the three rewrites and the listening
   debrief. Ask them which rewrite would actually work on *this* team, and
   whether your listening debrief sounds like you were solving too early.
   Add three lines to the notes from that conversation.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Leadership notes have a dated ticket 2 section with Start with Heart,
      pool of shared meaning, and the four listening moves in your words.
- [ ] Three vague messages are rewritten with owner, outcome, time, and a
      chosen channel. One of them is a real or recently used message.
- [ ] You ran one real listening conversation (or a mentor conversation if
      no teammate was available), reflected back, asked an open question,
      and wrote a debrief.
- [ ] You closed one coordination loop with an explicit who / what / when
      sentence you can show in the notes.
- [ ] A mentor has seen the rewrites and the listening debrief. This
      website does not have those notes.

## Notes

- Reflecting back is not mockery and not a stall. If it feels robotic,
  use their words: "You're worried the belt will walk because the shaft
  isn't retaining — did I get that?"
- Public call-outs in chat ("why isn't this done") feel efficient and
  usually cost you influence. Same content, private channel, specific ask.
- Do not record a teammate or paste their private messages into a shared
  folder. Your notes describe *your* behavior.
- The next ticket, [Small-Team Collaboration](../small-team-collaboration/),
  uses this listening habit to set a shared goal, name owners, and write
  a RACI for one subsystem. Bring the same notes.
