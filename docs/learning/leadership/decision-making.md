## Description

Senior leaders on a volunteer team are paid in trust to decide — often
with a missing part, a half-written scout sheet, and a match in
seventeen days. Decide too slowly and the shop stalls while people wait
for the titled person. Decide recklessly and you spend influence you
cannot buy back. A captain who flips a design every night trains the
team to stop investing. A captain who never calls it trains the team to
stop asking.

You already know how to run a room (ticket 5), name stakeholders
(ticket 7), and tell important from merely loud (ticket 9). This ticket
is a **process you can show**, not a vibe that you are "decisive."
Annie Duke's *Thinking in Bets* is the useful correction: a good
decision is one that was sound given what you knew, not one that got
lucky. You will write the decision that way so a bad bounce does not
turn into a character trial.

A simple path:

1. Frame the real question and the decide-by time.
2. Gather *enough* information — perfect information is a stall.
3. Generate at least two options, including "do nothing."
4. Weigh them against criteria (time, risk, cost, mission).
5. Decide and commit out loud.
6. Communicate what, why, and who is affected.
7. Review the outcome later without rewriting history.

For recurring problems, ask **why** until you hit a cause you can
change (the 5 Whys). A one-time fix and a process change are different
jobs. The electrical troubleshooting mindset — isolate, test, verify —
is the same discipline on hardware; use it if you have that ticket.

Match involvement to the door. **Reversible** (two-way) decisions can
be cheap and fast. **One-way doors** (cutting a unique tube, sending
the Impact essay, picking an alliance-level strategy) need more voices
and more time. Decide alone when the stakes are small. Consult, then
decide, when you need information. Build consensus when the team must
live with an irreversible call. That is not weakness. That is how
unpaid people stay in the boat.

This site does not track whether you finished. The decision memo and
the 5 Whys live in your Leadership notes.

## What you'll learn

- A seven-step decision you can write down and defend.
- How to use 5 Whys on a recurring shop or team problem.
- How to pick decide-alone vs consult vs consensus, and two-way vs
  one-way doors.
- How to own the outcome without blaming the volunteers who executed
  a call you made.

## Tasks

1. **Read deciding-under-uncertainty, short.** Read a concise overview
   of Annie Duke's idea that decisions are bets — her
   [Thinking in Bets](https://www.annieduke.com/thinking-in-bets/)
   page is enough if you do not have the book; read chapter 1 if you
   do. Then read MindTools'
   [5 Whys](https://www.mindtools.com/a3mi00v/5-whys).
   Optional:
   [Electrical: Systematic Troubleshooting](../../electrical/troubleshooting/)
   for the same isolate-and-test habit on hardware.

2. **Write a decision memo for a live call.** Pick a real decision
   your group must make in the next two weeks (mechanism geometry,
   which auto to keep, whether to staff an outreach event, whether to
   cut a feature). In a dated `Ticket 10 — Decisions` section, walk
   all seven steps. The memo should be something you could hand a
   mentor who was not in the room. Include the decide-by date from
   ticket 7's commitment style.

3. **Classify three other decisions.** List three calls your team made
   or will make. For each: reversible or one-way; decide-alone /
   consult / consensus; who you would actually involve (use the
   stakeholder map and the RACI). If a past call used the wrong
   involvement level, say so.

4. **Run 5 Whys on a recurring problem.** Pick something that has
   happened at least twice (lost tools, late standups, a sensor that
   "just dies," a document nobody updates). Write at least five why
   layers. Name whether the fix is a one-time patch or a process
   change (a checkout bin, a standup timer, a strain-relief standard).
   Do one small piece of that fix this week, or assign it using
   ticket 6's brief.

5. **Make the call and communicate it.** For the memo in Task 2, make
   or facilitate the decision by the date you wrote. Communicate it
   where the team looks (standup, chat, a note on the board): what was
   decided, why, who is affected, and the first action item. Do not
   hide behind "we should probably…" If you are not the Accountable
   person, your job is to force the question onto their calendar, not
   to steal the A.

6. **Review and mentor conversation.** After some evidence is in (a
   day is enough for a small call), write: given what we knew, was
   the *process* sound? What luck or bad luck showed up? Show a mentor
   the memo and the 5 Whys. Ask them which one-way doors this team
   has treated like two-way doors. Add a paragraph.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Notes include a seven-step decision memo for a live call, written
      so a mentor who missed the meeting can follow it.
- [ ] Three other decisions are classified by reversibility and
      involvement level, with names of who should be in the room.
- [ ] A 5 Whys write-up on a recurring problem names a one-time fix vs
      a process change, and you started that change or delegated it.
- [ ] The live decision was communicated with what / why / who / next
      action.
- [ ] A short after-action in the notes separates process quality from
      outcome luck.
- [ ] A mentor reviewed the memo. Work stays in your Leadership notes,
      not on this website.

## Notes

- Analysis can be avoidance. If you are still gathering data after the
  decide-by date you wrote, you decided to stall. Write that down.
- Owning a bad outcome means "the call was mine," not "the freshman
  executed it wrong" in front of the group. Private SBI is still
  available (ticket 4).
- Consensus on a two-way door is how you waste a Saturday. Speed is a
  kindness when the decision is cheap to reverse.
- The next ticket, [Building Team Culture & Motivation](../team-culture-motivation/),
  is what your decisions *teach* the room: what you tolerate, what you
  celebrate, and whether volunteers still want to be here in March.
