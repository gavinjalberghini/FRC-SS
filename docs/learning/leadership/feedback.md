## Description

A volunteer team improves only as fast as people will tell each other the
truth. If the only feedback is "good job" and silent resentment, the robot
and the culture both stall. If the only feedback is a public roast after a
bad match, people hide. You now have influence-over-title (ticket 1),
clear-and-kind speech (ticket 2), and a RACI plus some psychological
safety (ticket 3). This ticket is the first time you use those on a
**specific person**, about a **specific behavior**, out loud.

Kim Scott's **Radical Candor** is a useful map: Care Personally and
Challenge Directly at the same time. Miss the care and you get **obnoxious
aggression** (the "I'm just being honest" teardown at the toolbox). Miss
the challenge and you get **ruinous empathy** (you like them, so you never
say the wires will fail inspection). Miss both and you get **manipulative
insincerity** (nice to their face, harsh in the group chat). Radical
Candor is not a license to be a jerk with a framework. It is kind, clear,
specific, and sincere — the same tone Gracious Professionalism asks for
when the work is sloppy and the person still matters.

A reliable sentence structure is **Situation → Behavior → Impact** (SBI),
taught widely by the Center for Creative Leadership:

1. **Situation** — "In Thursday's build meeting…"
2. **Behavior** — "…you said the bracket was done and did not mention the
   missing rivets…"
3. **Impact** — "…so electrical waited an hour on a part that could not
   mount."

Then you talk about the fix. Use the same skeleton for praise. "Your
labeling on the PDP made the FTA's life easy" trains the behavior you
want repeated. "You're a star" trains nothing.

Receiving is the harder half. How you take the first piece of criticism
decides whether anyone offers you a second. Listen, do not defend in the
first thirty seconds, ask for an example if it is vague, say thank you,
then decide what is true. You do not have to swallow a wrong story. You
do have to look for the kernel.

This site does not track whether you finished. The practice conversation
and the reflection stay in your Leadership notes. A mentor hears about
them from you.

## Prerequisites

## What you'll learn

- The Radical Candor map: care plus challenge, and the three ways teams
  fake it.
- How to give corrective feedback and praise with Situation → Behavior →
  Impact.
- How to receive feedback without a reflexive defense.
- How to keep corrective feedback private and praise specific and public.

## Tasks

1. **Read a Radical Candor overview, not a tweet thread.** Read Kim Scott's
   [What is Radical Candor?](https://www.radicalcandor.com/blog/what-is-radical-candor)
   and the
   [Radical Candor framework / approach](https://www.radicalcandor.com/our-approach).
   Then read CCL's
   [SBI feedback tool](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbi/).
   If a mentor has *Thanks for the Feedback* by Stone and Heen, skim the
   opening on "know vs. do" — receiving is a skill. Optional: the
   [code-review ticket](../../programming/code-review-leadership/) if you
   are a programmer; the same rules apply to a PR comment.

2. **Map your team's habits.** In a dated `Ticket 4 — Feedback` section of
   your Leadership notes, write one real example (no pile-on, no names if
   it would shame someone) of each:

   - ruinous empathy (something important that went unsaid)
   - obnoxious aggression (challenge without care)
   - Radical Candor you have seen, even once

   Then write which quadrant *you* fall into when you are tired. Be
   honest. Ticket 1's composure trait is doing work here.

3. **Draft two SBI scripts, then have one real conversation.** In the
   notes, write:

   - one **corrective** SBI about a behavior you actually observed (late
     to an owned RACI line, a unsafe shortcut, talking over a freshman).
     Keep it one behavior, not a personality review.
   - one **praise** SBI about a different person, specific enough that
     they know what to repeat.

   Then **practice the corrective conversation** with the real person if
   it is safe and proportionate — or with a mentor playing the teammate if
   the real conversation would be about harassment, safety, or something
   a student should not handle alone. Use Start with Heart from ticket 2:
   what do you want for them and for the robot? Private setting. No
   audience. Afterward, write what you said, what they said, and what you
   would change. The praise SBI should be delivered for real, in public
   if that fits, this week.

4. **Receive one piece of feedback.** Ask a mentor or a teammate: "What is
   one thing I should do differently in this group?" When they answer:

   - do not explain yourself in the first response
   - ask one clarifying question ("Can you give me a time that happened?")
   - say thank you
   - write the kernel you will act on, even if you disagree with 30% of it

   If they say "you're doing great," ask again: "Something small I don't
   see."

5. **Run a 15-minute retro, not a roast.** After a work session or a small
   milestone, take ten to fifteen minutes with your group: what went well,
   what to change, who owns the change. Use the listening moves. Park
   blame. Write three bullets in the notes. This is a warm-up for the
   standup you will run in the next ticket — keep it short on purpose.

6. **Mentor conversation.** Walk a mentor through the SBI you delivered
   (or the role-play) and the feedback you received. Ask whether you
   stayed on behavior and whether you made it safe. Add their notes under
   yours.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Notes include the three-quadrant map (ruinous empathy, obnoxious
      aggression, Radical Candor) with team-flavored examples and an
      honest self-quadrant when tired.
- [ ] You wrote one corrective SBI and one praise SBI. The praise was
      delivered to the real person.
- [ ] You practiced the corrective conversation with the real teammate or,
      if that was not appropriate, with a mentor in a role-play, and you
      wrote a debrief.
- [ ] You asked for feedback, did not defend in the first response, asked
      a clarifying question, thanked the giver, and wrote a kernel to act
      on.
- [ ] You facilitated a short retro (about 15 minutes) and captured three
      bullets in the notes.
- [ ] A mentor discussed the conversation with you. Work stays in your
      notes, not on this website.

## Notes

- Feedback about identity ("you're lazy," "you're a bad freshman") is not
  SBI and is not Gracious Professionalism. If you drafted that, rewrite it
  before you speak.
- Do not ambush someone in front of the drive team. Corrective feedback
  is private. If the issue is safety or harassment, skip the peer script
  and go to a mentor.
- Saving six weeks of irritation for "a real talk" is how volunteers quit.
  Timely and small beats theatrical and late.
- The next ticket, [Running Effective Meetings & Standups](../effective-meetings/),
  is where you run a real 15-minute standup with an agenda you keep. You
  will need the RACI from ticket 3 and the habit of not solving every
  problem in the circle.
