## Description

An FRC subsystem is usually five or fewer people, unpaid, under a deadline,
sharing one toolbox and one CAD document. That group can ship a working
intake in two weeks. It can also spend those two weeks duplicating work,
leaving the belt stack for "someone," and going quiet after a joke that
landed wrong. The difference is rarely talent. It is whether the group
shares one goal, whether every task has one owner, and whether it is safe
to say "I don't know" out loud.

Google's Project Aristotle studied what made teams effective and found
**psychological safety** at the top of the list — the shared belief that
you can ask a question, admit a mistake, or disagree without being
punished or embarrassed. Amy Edmondson defined it as safety for
interpersonal risk. On a volunteer team that risk is expensive. A freshman
who gets mocked for asking which way a bearing faces will not ask before
they press it on backwards. A programmer who is afraid to say the auto
is untested will smile through standup and fail the first match. Titles
cannot fix that. Influence and response-to-the-first-mistake can.

Ticket 1 said influence beats authority. Ticket 2 said listen, then be
clear. This ticket turns those habits into a small-group operating system:
one sentence for the current goal, one owner per task, work visible on a
board, and a RACI so "we" stops meaning "nobody."

A **RACI** names, for each deliverable, who is **R**esponsible (does the
work), **A**ccountable (one person who answers if it slips — never two),
**C**onsulted (asked before the decision), and **I**nformed (told after).
On a 20-person team you do not RACI the entire robot. You RACI *your*
subsystem for the next two weeks. You will reuse that chart when you
delegate in ticket 6 and when you run a standup in ticket 5.

Decide, then commit. Debate is healthy. Once the group picks a belt width,
you do not reopen it every time you walk past the table — unless new
evidence shows up. That is psychological safety plus discipline, not
silence.

This site does not track whether you finished. The RACI and the reflection
live in your Leadership notes. Mentors look at those with you.

## What you'll learn

- How to state a shared goal in one sentence and notice when the group
  does not share it.
- How a RACI prevents double work and ownerless work on a small subsystem.
- What psychological safety is, why Project Aristotle put it first, and
  two behaviors that build it this week.
- How a visible board and short syncs beat a long, rare meeting (the
  standup itself is the next ticket).

## Tasks

1. **Read the research, not a poster.** Read Google re:Work's
   [Understand team effectiveness](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness)
   (Project Aristotle). Then read Atlassian's
   [RACI chart](https://www.atlassian.com/work-management/project-management/raci-chart)
   far enough to explain Responsible vs Accountable in one breath. Optional:
   Amy Edmondson's short talk
   [How to turn a group of strangers into a team](https://www.ted.com/talks/amy_edmondson_how_to_turn_a_group_of_strangers_into_a_team)
   (about 13 minutes) if you want her voice on safety.

2. **Test the shared goal.** Ask two people in the group you named in
   ticket 1, separately: "What is this group trying to finish *this week*,
   in one sentence?" Write both answers and yours in the notes. If they
   do not match, that is the finding. Draft a one-sentence goal you will
   propose ("Have a driven roller on the practice bot that picks a note
   from the floor by Saturday 4 p.m."). You are not announcing a new
   dictatorship. You are offering a sentence the group can correct.

3. **Write a RACI for that subsystem.** In the Leadership notes, make a
   simple list or table for the **next two weeks only**. Include at least
   six lines, such as:

   - CAD of the current revision
   - parts pulled / ordered
   - assembly
   - wiring or sensor mount (if it applies)
   - software interface or bench test
   - documentation or a "how to run it" card for drive team

   For each line, fill R, A, C, and I with real names. **A must be one
   person.** If you do not know a name, write "unknown — ask mentor" rather
   than inventing an owner. Use ticket 2's clear language: a role is a
   person, not "mechanical."

4. **Make work visible.** Put those six items on a board the group already
   uses (whiteboard columns, a GitHub project, a sticky-note wall). If the
   group has no board, make a paper one for one week — To do / Doing /
   Blocked / Done — and photograph it for your notes (no faces required).
   Programming teammates can peek at
   [Kanban & Agile Practices](../../programming/kanban-agile/); you do not
   need that whole ticket to finish this one.

5. **Practice one safety behavior.** Pick two from this list and do them
   at a real session:

   - Admit a mistake of your own before anyone else has to find it.
   - Thank someone for raising a problem early, out loud.
   - Answer a "dumb" question with curiosity, not a sigh.
   - Invite a quiet person into a design choice: "What do you see?"

   Write what you did and how the room reacted. Gracious Professionalism
   is the FIRST name for not using competence as a weapon.

6. **Ticket 3 reflection and mentor pass.** In the notes, write:

   - whether the three goal sentences matched, and the sentence you will
     use going forward
   - who is Accountable for the riskiest line on your RACI, and why
   - two safety behaviors you practiced
   - one collaboration habit you will keep (visible work, pairing on a
     stuck task, sharing *why* not only *what*)

   Walk a mentor through the RACI. Ask them who is overloaded and who has
   no real A. Fix the chart after that conversation.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Notes include a dated ticket 3 section and a one-sentence current
      goal, checked against two teammates' answers.
- [ ] A RACI for your real subsystem covers at least six deliverables for
      the next two weeks, with a single A on every line and real names
      (or an explicit unknown).
- [ ] Those items exist on a visible board (digital or paper) your group
      can see.
- [ ] You practiced two psychological-safety behaviors at a real session
      and wrote what happened.
- [ ] You can explain Project Aristotle's finding on psychological safety
      in two sentences without this page.
- [ ] A mentor reviewed the RACI and you updated it from that conversation.
      The chart lives in your notes, not on this website.

## Notes

- Two Accountables is zero Accountables. If two leads both "own" wiring,
  the harness will be nobody's problem at 10 p.m.
- A RACI is not a way to dump grunt work on freshmen and keep design for
  titled people. Stretch people; do not set them up to fail.
- Psychological safety is not "everyone is comfortable." Disagreement
  about ideas should get easier, not quieter. Ticket 8 is where you
  practice the hard version.
- Keep other students' names in a document a mentor may read. Be fair.
  No gossip column.
- The next ticket, [Giving & Receiving Feedback](../feedback/), is how
  you tell a specific person what to change without lighting the safety
  you just built on fire.
