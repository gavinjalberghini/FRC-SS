## Description

Put passionate unpaid people under a hard deadline and they will
disagree — about a pivot vs a fixed roller, about who gets the only
good driver station, about a joke that was not a joke. Conflict is not
the failure. **Unmanaged** conflict is. A team with no disagreement is
often a team where it is unsafe to speak (ticket 3). A team with
unmanaged disagreement loses people, because volunteers can leave.

Healthy conflict is about **ideas**. Unhealthy conflict is about
**people**: character attacks, grudges, silence, or a group chat that
re-litigates a decision after the owner left the room. Your job as a
leader is to encourage the first and interrupt the second early,
privately, and assuming good intent until you have evidence otherwise.
Gracious Professionalism is the FIRST name for competing hard without
turning the other person into the enemy — including a teammate.

You already have Start with Heart and the pool of shared meaning
(ticket 2), SBI and Radical Candor (ticket 4), and a stakeholder map
(ticket 7). This ticket adds a repeatable path for when stakes, opinions,
and emotions are all high — the actual definition of a crucial
conversation — plus a mediator's posture when two teammates are stuck.

Fisher and Ury's *Getting to Yes* adds one more distinction you will
use out loud: **positions** ("we have to use a 2-inch roller") vs
**interests** ("we need a note to stay captured on a bump"). Interests
often fit together. Positions fight.

Some things are not yours to mediate. Harassment, discrimination,
safety violations, and repeated blow-ups after you have tried go to a
mentor or the designated adult. That is judgment. It is not a failed
leadership ticket.

This site does not track whether you finished. The write-up of the
conversation you ran (or the structured role-play) stays in your
Leadership notes.

## What you'll learn

- How to tell healthy idea-conflict from unhealthy person-conflict.
- A step-by-step way to work a disagreement you are *in*.
- How to mediate two teammates without picking a mascot.
- When to escalate to a mentor instead of "owning it."

## Tasks

1. **Read the hard-conversation ideas.** Read (or reread)
   [Start with Heart](https://cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-start-with-heart/)
   and the glossary entries for *Fool's Choice*, *Mutual Purpose*, and
   *Pool of Shared Meaning* on
   [Crucial Learning's glossary](https://cruciallearning.com/glossary/).
   If a copy of *Crucial Conversations* is available, read "Make It
   Safe" as well. Then read a short *Getting to Yes* overview — the
   [Program on Negotiation's Getting to Yes page](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/shop/getting-to-yes/)
   plus any mentor-owned copy of the interests-vs-positions chapters.
   Finish with FIRST's
   [Gracious Professionalism](https://www.firstinspires.org/about)
   language. Write, in the notes, four sentences: mutual purpose,
   fool's choice, interests vs positions, GP in a design argument.

2. **Classify a real disagreement.** In a dated `Ticket 8 — Conflict`
   section, describe a recent disagreement on your team (design,
   priority, workload, or tone). Do not write a hit piece. Cover:

   - was it healthy (ideas) or unhealthy (people), and how you can tell
   - the official **positions**
   - the likely **interests** underneath
   - what would have happened if someone had addressed it the day it
     started instead of hoping

3. **Walk the resolution method on paper first.** Using that same
   conflict (or a smaller live one), write how each step would sound
   in your voice:

   1. Cool down if it is hot — you do not problem-solve mid-yell.
   2. Name shared ground ("we both want a legal, reliable intake by
      Week 5").
   3. Separate people from the problem — attack the issue.
   4. Understand before persuading — each side talks; the other
      reflects back (ticket 2) until they feel heard.
   5. Interests, not positions — ask *why* until the need is visible.
   6. Invent options, pick one, set a check that it worked.

   This is the script. You will not read it like a play, but you will
   not invent the path in the moment either.

4. **Run the conversation or a structured role-play.** Preferred: a
   real, proportionate disagreement you are in or can facilitate this
   week (belt vs roller, which auto to keep, who stays late). Use a
   private space. Start with Heart. If the conflict is too charged,
   stale, or about harm, **do not freelance** — role-play with a
   mentor and a second student using a realistic shop scenario. If you
   are mediating two teammates: they talk to each other, not through
   you; you stay neutral; one person at a time; no character attacks;
   you land a written agreement (who does what by when — ticket 5's
   action-item format).

5. **Write the debrief.** In the notes, after the conversation:

   - what mutual purpose you named
   - one interest that was not obvious from the starting positions
   - the agreement, copied as it was said
   - whether you stayed out of "winning"
   - what you would escalate next time instead of handling yourself
     (name categories: safety, harassment, repeated refusal, anything
     that needs an adult)

6. **Mentor conversation.** Walk the mentor through the debrief and
   the escalation list. Ask them how *this* team has handled a past
   conflict well and badly. Add a paragraph. If a relationship still
   needs repair, agree with the mentor on one follow-up, then do that
   follow-up — a check that the agreement held, not a new trial.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Notes include four-sentence takeaways on mutual purpose, fool's
      choice, interests vs positions, and Gracious Professionalism.
- [ ] You classified a real team disagreement as healthy or unhealthy
      and named positions vs interests.
- [ ] You wrote the six-step method in your own voice for that
      conflict.
- [ ] You ran a real resolution conversation or a mentor-structured
      role-play, and you captured the agreement in writing.
- [ ] You listed conflict types you will escalate to a mentor rather
      than own as a student.
- [ ] A mentor reviewed the debrief. The write-up stays in your
      Leadership notes, not on this website.

## Notes

- Addressing it early and privately is not drama. Letting it go public
  is. The group chat is a terrible courtroom.
- Neutrality is not "both sides are equally right." It is "I will not
  humiliate either person while we get to a decision."
- If you are a party to the conflict, you probably should not also be
  the mediator. Get a mentor or a third student who has finished
  ticket 4.
- Repair matters. A clean decision with two people who will not look
  at each other is a partial failure. Check back once.
- The next ticket, [Time & Priority Management](../time-priority-management/),
  is how many conflicts never start: the team is arguing because
  everything is "urgent" and nothing is sequenced.
