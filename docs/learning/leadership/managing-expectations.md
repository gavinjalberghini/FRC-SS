## Description

Most anger in an FRC shop is not about the failed part. It is about the
story people were told. Drive team expected a camera on Thursday because
someone said "should be fine." A sponsor expected a banner at the event
because someone said "we're on it." A parent expected pickup at 8:00
because the calendar still said 8:00 after the meeting slipped. On a
volunteer team those gaps do not get a performance review. They get a
quiet decision not to come next week.

**Managing expectations** is closing the gap between what people believe
will happen and what you can actually deliver — then keeping that gap
closed when reality moves. Ticket 5 gave you owned action items. Ticket 6
gave you a handoff. This ticket is the honesty layer: estimate with
buffer, name the trade-off when you cannot have scope and time and
quality, know who is waiting on you, and deliver bad news while there is
still time to solve it.

A **stakeholder** is anyone depending on the work: another subsystem,
drive team, mentors, the lead mentor who promised an inspector a
practice-bot demo, a sponsor, the student who needs the robot to film a
recruiting reel. You do not owe every stakeholder every want. You do owe
them a clear need-versus-want conversation and a proactive update when
the plan changes.

When something will slip, early bad news is a problem. Late bad news is
a crisis. Bring impact and a proposed plan, not a confession with no
next step. That is servant leadership under deadline: you serve the
people who planned around you, including the ones with no title.

This site does not track whether you finished. The stakeholder list, the
buffered commitment, and the bad-news script live in your Leadership
notes. A mentor hears the practice conversation.

## What you'll learn

- How to turn an optimistic "we can do that" into a buffered, concrete
  commitment.
- How to name a scope / time / quality trade-off with the people who
  will feel it.
- How to list stakeholders and separate their needs from their wants.
- How to deliver a delay early, in person, with impact and a plan.

## Tasks

1. **Read a short project-honesty piece.** If you can borrow Scott
   Berkun's *Making Things Happen*, read the chapters on schedules and
   saying no. If not, read Berkun's essay
   [How to give a status report](https://scottberkun.com/essays/23-how-to-give-a-status-report/)
   and FIRST's
   [About](https://www.firstinspires.org/about)
   page again — Gracious Professionalism includes not lighting people up
   when you are the one who slipped. Optional: your team's season
   roadmap or event calendar, so the dates in this ticket are real.

2. **Turn one optimistic date into a commitment.** Take a live item from
   your RACI, your last standup agenda, or the task you delegated in
   ticket 6. In the notes, write:

   - the **optimistic** estimate you (or someone) first said out loud
   - what could go wrong (missing part, school concert, robot in pieces
     for a photo)
   - the **buffered** commitment: what, by when, to what quality
   - what you will *not* promise

   Then tell the person who was waiting the buffered version, even if it
   is less heroic than the original sentence. Under-promise, over-deliver
   is how volunteers keep trusting you.

3. **Name the trade-off.** Pick a project currently under pressure
   (bumpers, auto, a reveal video, a budget line). In the notes, write
   which lever you would move with stakeholders — **cut scope**, **move
   the date**, or **lower quality** — and why. For robot safety and
   inspection items, "lower quality" is usually the wrong lever. Say
   that explicitly if it applies. Draft the two-sentence version you
   would say to the group: "We can have A by Saturday if we drop B."

4. **Stakeholder map for your group.** List at least five stakeholders
   for the work you actually do. For each: what they **need** by when,
   what they **want** that is extra, and how you will update them
   (standup, a mentor, a text to the drive coach). Circle the person
   who would be most damaged by a surprise. That is who gets the early
   call.

5. **Practice delivering bad news.** Write a script in the notes using
   ticket 2 and ticket 4 (Start with Heart, then facts):

   - the slip or cut, said in the first two sentences
   - impact on the robot / event / people
   - your proposed plan and what you need from them
   - a question: "Does that still meet the need, or do we cut something
     else?"

   Deliver it to a **real stakeholder** if a real slip exists this week.
   If nothing is slipping, role-play with a mentor using a recent near
   miss ("we almost told drive team the camera was ready"). Do not
   practice only in your head.

6. **Mentor conversation and reflection.** Show the mentor the buffered
   commitment, the stakeholder map, and the script you used. Ask them
   who on this team is chronically surprised and why. In the notes:

   - what you used to hide or hope
   - what you will say earlier next time
   - how this changes the next standup action-item dates

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Notes include a dated ticket 7 section with an optimistic estimate
      rewritten as a concrete, buffered commitment you actually
      communicated.
- [ ] You named a scope / time / quality trade-off for a live project
      and wrote the two-sentence group version.
- [ ] A stakeholder map lists at least five real people or roles, with
      need vs want and an update channel.
- [ ] You delivered a bad-news conversation (real slip or mentor
      role-play) that led with the news, impact, and a plan.
- [ ] A mentor reviewed the map and the script. Work stays in your
      Leadership notes, not on this website.

## Notes

- "Mostly done" and "soon" are not commitments. If you cannot name a
  clock time and a done picture, you do not have an agreement yet.
- Hiding a slip to "not stress people" is ruinous empathy (ticket 4)
  at team scale. They still find out. They just find out with no
  options.
- Mentors and parents are stakeholders too. A calendar lie costs more
  than a late robot feature.
- The next ticket, [Conflict Resolution](../conflict-resolution/), is
  what happens when two stakeholders needed different things and the
  conversation got hot. Bring Start with Heart and the stakeholder map.
