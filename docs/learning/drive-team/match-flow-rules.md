## Overview

You can drive well and still lose to a rule. Starting configuration, zone
lines, human-player timing, and contact rules are part of driving. This
lesson is the match clock and the field rules a crew member must know
without flipping the manual mid-queue.

## Prerequisites

- [Drive Team Roles](../drive-team-roles/)
- This season’s Game Manual match-play section

## What you'll learn

- The phases of a match and what the robot may do in each.
- Common ways legal robots become illegal on the field.
- How human-player and starting-position rules constrain the driver.

## Unit 1 — The clock

A typical match is **autonomous**, then **teleop**, with an **endgame**
window that is a rule and a strategy, not just “the last seconds.”

- Auto is a program. Drivers do not “help.” Hands off.
- Teleop starts when the field says, not when you feel ready.
- Endgame has a start-you-can-survive. Leaving a cycle too late is how
  you neither score nor climb.

Know this season’s exact times. They move.

## Unit 2 — Rules that catch good drivers

- **Starting configuration and size** — if it unfolds illegally, you
  never enabled a legal robot.
- **Zones and contact** — pin, herding, launching, and bumper-zone rules
  change every year. The coach watches these so the driver can drive.
- **Game-piece rules** — carrying limits, launch zones, what the human
  player may touch.

If you are not sure, ask before the match, not after the card.

## Unit 3 — Field staff are part of the match

- FTA and referees are not opponents. Short, factual answers.
- If you disagree with a call, the coach handles it after the match
  through the question box — not from the station during a live robot.
- Never step onto the field until you are told.

## Steps & acceptance criteria

- [ ] Recite this season’s auto / teleop / endgame times.
- [ ] Name three rules that would card or foul a sloppy driver.
- [ ] Draw legal starting positions for your robot on a field sketch.
- [ ] Explain who talks to a referee, and when.
