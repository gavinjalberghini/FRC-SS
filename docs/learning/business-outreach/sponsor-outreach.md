## Description

The package is worthless if nobody sends it — and the check is wasted if
you disappear until next January. This ticket is the working loop: write,
meet, deliver, report. You already know
[what a sponsor actually buys](../sponsorship-fundamentals/) and you have a
research card in the team’s drive. Now you will write the email a human
will finish.

If you have never asked anyone for money, the fear is normal. The fix is
not a longer letter. The fix is a short letter that reuses the
[one-sentence pitch](../team-story-brand/), points at one
[budget line](../season-budget/), and names one benefit you can keep.
Students sign it. Mentors may review; they should not ghostwrite. A
letter that sounds like a brochure gets filed with the brochures.

Keep the **ask** to one page, or a short email with the same bones:

1. Who you are (the pitch from Lesson 2).
2. What you did last season that a stranger can picture (a number, a
   named school, a named robot function — not “we had a great year”).
3. The specific ask and what it funds (the line from the research card).
4. The benefit you will deliver, and by when.
5. A clear next step (a date, a phone number the team owns, a visit).

Nonprofit writers will tell you the same thing: a person, a problem, a
next step. Skim
[GoFundMe Pro on nonprofit storytelling](https://pro.gofundme.com/c/blog/nonprofit-storytelling/)
and, for the thank-you that comes later,
[DonorPerfect on impact stories](https://www.donorperfect.com/nonprofit-technology-blog/fundraising-software/impact-stories-nonprofit-storytelling/).
You are not writing a donation page. You are stealing the shape.

The **meeting** is a 20-minute conversation, not a pitch deck recital:

- Two students, one adult in the background. Students lead.
- Bring the robot or a mechanism if you can; photos if you cannot.
- Ask what *they* care about before you pitch the gold tier. The
  research card is a hypothesis. The meeting tests it.
- Write down every promise before you leave the parking lot.

If they say no, thank them and ask who else they would introduce you to.
A polite no is still a relationship. FIRST’s
[Fundraising Toolkit](https://www.firstinspires.org/resources/library/fundraising-toolkit)
puts “the ask” and “thank you” in that order for a reason.

**Stewardship** is the season after the check — how you earn the next one:

- Send the photo of their logo on the robot the week it goes on.
- Invite them to a practice or an event with a specific time, not “come
  anytime.”
- Close the year with a one-page report: what their money did, who it
  reached, what is next.
- Keep a contact log in the **team’s drive** so a graduating lead does
  not take the relationship with them.

Adapting the message to the audience is a leadership skill as much as a
business one. If the letter feels stiff,
[Communication Fundamentals](../../leadership/communication-fundamentals/)
is the adjacent ticket.

**This website does not store sponsor lists, draft emails, or contact
logs.** The draft lives in the team’s drive. Mentors review a link. If
your team exported these tickets, the issue gets the link, not the
prospect’s personal email pasted in public.

## What you'll learn

- How to write a short outreach letter that a human will finish.
- How to run a 20-minute sponsor meeting with students talking most of
  the minutes.
- How stewardship works after the money arrives — and where the log
  lives so it survives graduation.

## Tasks

1. **Read the ask-and-thank sequence.** Skim the Ask and Thank You
   steps on the
   [Fundraising Toolkit](https://www.firstinspires.org/resources/library/fundraising-toolkit)
   and the “lead with a clear problem / name a next step” section of
   [Nonprofit storytelling](https://pro.gofundme.com/c/blog/nonprofit-storytelling/).
   Write five headings in a doc: Who / Last season / Ask / Benefit /
   Next step. Those are the letter.

2. **Write the draft email (or one-page letter).** Address it to the
   prospect on your research card. Reuse the pitch sentence. Name the
   budget line and the number. Name the benefit and a date you can
   keep. Sign it with **student names** and one team-owned contact
   (team email or mentor phone the adult owner approves). Keep it under
   300 words. Put the draft in the team drive next to the card.

3. **Get one markup.** A lead or mentor marks the draft once. You
   accept or reject each comment in writing on the doc. A second
   rewrite that adds adjectives is a miss. A second rewrite that cuts
   them is the job.

4. **Role-play the meeting.** Twenty minutes. Two students lead. One
   adult or lead plays the prospect and asks at least one of: “What
   exactly do you need the money for?”, “What did last year’s sponsor
   get?”, and “What happens if we say no?” Students talk at least
   80% of the minutes. Afterward, write down every promise you made
   in the role-play. If you promised something that is not on the
   package, cut it from the real meeting.

5. **Draft the stewardship update.** For an **existing** supporter
   (or last year’s, if you have no current one), write a short email
   that would go out this week: one photo or one number, one sentence
   about what their help did, one specific invitation with a date.
   If you cannot send it yet, queue it with the adult owner. Sending
   is better than queuing. Ghosting is not a third option.

6. **Update the contact record.** In the team drive (the same place
   as the research cards), add or update one record with: contact
   name, organization, last conversation date, amount or in-kind (if
   any), promised benefits, and who on the team owns the relationship.
   This website does not get a copy.

7. **Hand the links to a mentor.** Draft email, markup, role-play
   notes, stewardship draft, contact record. If they approve the
   ask, they — not this page — decide when it sends. If your team
   exported these tickets, paste the drive links on the issue.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] A one-page ask or short email exists for a real prospect, uses
      the five-part structure, and a lead has marked it up once.
- [ ] The ask names a budget line and a benefit with a date.
- [ ] Students led a 20-minute role-play and talked at least 80% of
      the minutes. A mentor or lead watched or played the prospect.
- [ ] A stewardship update is sent, or drafted and queued with an
      adult owner, to an existing supporter.
- [ ] One sponsor record in the **team’s drive** has contact, amount
      or in-kind, promised benefits, and a student owner.
- [ ] No prospect list or personal email was stored on this website.

## Notes

- Do not send the real email from a personal Gmail if the team has a
  team-owned address. Graduation deletes inboxes.
- Do not put a student’s personal cell in the letter unless a mentor
  and a parent have both said yes. Use a team channel.
- A “no” with a referral is a win. Log it. Do not argue.
- Youth protection still applies in a sponsor’s conference room. Read
  [FIRST Youth Protection](https://www.firstinspires.org/programs/youth-protection-program)
  if two students and one adult is not obvious yet.
- The next ticket ([Planning Outreach Events](../outreach-events/)) is
  how you design the shop visit or community hour you just promised.
