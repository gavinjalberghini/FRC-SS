## Description

Two robots can post the same cycle count and be very different partners.
**Qualitative scouting** — sometimes called super scouting — captures
driving, defense, reliability, and "would we want this robot next to
us." This ticket is how to write notes that a pick-list meeting can use
without turning the sheet into politics.

You already have quantitative sheets from
[Match Scouting](../match-scouting/)
and capability cards from
[Pit Scouting](../pit-scouting/).
Qualitative does not override a sample of ten matches without a reason.
It explains the sample. "They're good" is not an explanation.
"Swerve, consistent hub bursts, 0/3 climbs, driver overcommits on
defense, drew a foul in Q12" is.

Write like a reporter: one or two sentences, a match key, a fact you
could rewind and show a skeptic. If you cannot point at a match, it is
a rumor. The scout lead edits slurs, jokes, and unprovable claims out
of the sheet before the pick meeting. Separate "we don't like their
team culture" from "their robot failed twice." Culture can matter for
an alliance; do not disguise it as a cycle count.

What usually changes a pick without changing average score:

- **Driving** — smooth vs. crashes, field awareness, can they play
  defense without fouling. A high-cycle robot that cannot share a
  side is a second-pick problem.
- **Intake / mechanism reliability** — jams, recovery time, "they
  stop trying after two misses." Your cycle mean will hide this if
  they popped off in one match.
- **Defense** — effective vs. just in the way; do they draw cards.
  A defender who gifts 15-point majors is a negative cycle from
  [Scoring, Cycles & Penalties](../scoring-and-cycles/).
- **Human players and coaches** — chaos in the station shows up on
  the field. You can see some of it from the stands.
- **Repair culture** — same failure every match, or a pit that
  actually fixes it by the next one. Pit cards from
  [Pit Scouting](../pit-scouting/)
  are the claim; this is the pattern.

This is a short ticket on purpose. The skill is the sentence, not a
new spreadsheet. Super scouting is not a license to hash cycles for
six robots in the same pass — you already did numbers. Keep notes in
the same store as your match sheets. This website does not collect
them.

## What you'll learn

- Which qualities change a pick without changing a team's average
  score.
- How to write a note that is specific, match-cited, and short.
- How to keep qualitative from becoming a popularity contest.

## Tasks

1. **Watch the shared match for all six robots.** Replay
   [2026 Einstein Finals 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EjF9we707DA)
   with
   [the TBA box score](https://www.thebluealliance.com/match/2026cmptx_f1m1)
   open. You already timed or tally-scouted *one* robot. Now write
   qualitative notes for **all six** (4065, 4414, 1323, 868, 2910,
   2046) in this style — one or two sentences each, match key
   `2026cmptx_f1m1`:

   > 2910: swerve, stayed in a scoring role through shifts, no tower
   > attempt visible, defense was positional not card-hunting.

   Cover, as they apply: driving (smooth / crashes / field awareness),
   intake or mechanism reliability (jams, recovery, "stopped trying"),
   defense (effective vs. just in the way; cards), human players and
   coaches if you can see the station, and whether a failure looked
   like a pit that will fix it or a pattern.

2. **Convert one vague comment.** Take a real sentence you have heard
   on your team — or invent the one you will hear ("they're trash,"
   "pretty robot," "first pick for sure"). Rewrite it as a specific,
   match-cited note, **or** discard it and write "discarded — no
   match evidence." See
   [Leadership: Feedback]({{ '/learning/leadership/feedback/' | relative_url }})
   if you need the difference between specific and personal.

3. **List two qualities that move a pick without moving average
   score.** Examples that usually work: they play defense without
   fouling; they have a second auto; they break the same chain every
   match; their driver cannot share a side; they will not talk in the
   alliance station. Write why each would move a team **up** or
   **down** a list you will build in
   [Building a Pick List](../pick-lists/).
   Tie at least one to your
   [Robot Priorities](../robot-priorities/)
   role sentence ("we need a partner who can…").

4. **Edit a dirty note.** Write the worst version of a note you must
   never bring to a meeting (insult, joke, rumor about a person). Then
   write the scout-lead edit: fact, match, or delete. The lead's job
   is the second sentence.

5. **File the six notes with the match sheets.** Same folder, same
   date, match key in the filename or header. If your team exported
   these tickets, paste a link to that folder on the issue and move it
   to In Review. A mentor should be able to open Einstein Finals 1
   sheets and qualitative notes side by side without asking you where
   the vibes went.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] Qualitative notes exist for all six robots in Einstein Finals 1
      (or a mentor-approved six-robot match), each one or two
      sentences with the match key.
- [ ] One vague teammate-style comment was rewritten with a citation
      or explicitly discarded.
- [ ] Two qualities are listed that would move a pick without changing
      average score, and at least one cites your alliance role.
- [ ] A "dirty note → edited note" pair exists so a mentor can see you
      know the line.
- [ ] Notes live with your match sheets, not only in chat, and a
      mentor can open them next to the Einstein sheets.

## Notes

- Timestamp the match. "Sometime on Friday" is how rumors become
  first picks.
- Super scouting is not a license to scout two robots at once for
  numbers. If you are writing qualitative for six, you are not also
  hashing cycles for six in the same pass.
- Next:
  [Data Analysis](../data-analysis/).
  You will put numbers, TBA, and Statbotics on the same card as these
  sentences — and you will not let EPA overwrite a jammed intake.
