## Overview

Two robots can post the same cycle count and be very different partners.
Qualitative scouting — sometimes called super scouting — captures driving,
defense, reliability, and “would we want this robot next to us.” This lesson
is how to write notes that a pick-list meeting can use.

## Prerequisites

- [Match Scouting](../match-scouting/)
- [Pit Scouting](../pit-scouting/)

## What you'll learn

- Which qualities change a pick.
- How to write a note that is specific, not vibes.
- How to keep qualitative from becoming politics.

## Unit 1 — What to watch besides scores

- **Driving** — smooth, crashes, field awareness, can they play defense
  without fouling.
- **Intake / mechanism reliability** — jams, recovery time, “they stop
  trying after two misses.”
- **Defense** — effective vs. just in the way; do they draw cards.
- **Human players and coaches** — chaos in the station shows up on the
  field.
- **Repair culture** — same failure every match, or a pit that actually
  fixes it.

## Unit 2 — Write like a reporter

Bad: “They’re good.” “They’re trash.” “Pretty robot.”

Good: “Swerve, consistent L4, 0/3 climbs, driver overcommits on defense,
drew a foul in Q12.”

One or two sentences. Timestamp the match. If you cannot point at a match,
it is a rumor.

## Unit 3 — Keep it honest

- Qualitative does not override a sample of 10 matches without a reason.
- Separate “we don’t like their team culture” from “their robot failed
  twice.” Culture can matter for an alliance; do not disguise it as
  scouting.
- The scout lead edits slurs, jokes, and unprovable claims out of the
  sheet before the pick meeting.

## Steps & acceptance criteria

- [ ] Watch one match and write qualitative notes for all six robots in
      the Unit 2 style.
- [ ] Convert one vague teammate comment into a specific, match-cited
      note — or discard it.
- [ ] List two qualities that would move a team up or down a pick list
      without changing their average score.
