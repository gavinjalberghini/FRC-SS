## Overview

Match scouting is a measurement process. If two scouts mean different things
by “cycle,” the spreadsheet is fiction. This lesson is definitions, attention,
and the habits that keep Friday’s data usable on Saturday morning.

## Prerequisites

- [Designing a Scouting System](../scouting-system-design/)

## What you'll learn

- How to write a one-page data dictionary.
- How to watch one robot without chasing the ball.
- How to catch bad rows before they hit the pick list.

## Unit 1 — The data dictionary

For every quantitative field, write one sentence:

- When it increments (game piece *scored and counted*, not “attempted”).
- What does *not* count (a drop, a foul-negated score, a climb that did
  not latch).
- Who resolves ties (scout lead, not an argument in the stands).

Train on one recorded match until five scouts produce the same numbers ±1.

## Unit 2 — Watching the robot

- Pick a robot before the match, not “whoever has the piece.”
- Watch bumpers. If you lose them, skip the cycle — do not invent it.
- Mark disabled, no-show, and major fouls even when the rest of the sheet
  is empty. Those rows matter.

Phones for the form are fine. Phones for chat during a match are how you
miss the climb.

## Unit 3 — Quality control

- A second pair of eyes on the first two matches of the day.
- TBA or audience display as a sanity check on *final score*, not on
  per-robot cycles.
- Delete or quarantine rows from a scout who is consistently off after
  retraining — quietly, then help them.

## Steps & acceptance criteria

- [ ] Write a data dictionary for this season’s form.
- [ ] Scout the same recorded match as two teammates; reconcile
      differences.
- [ ] Scout six robots across two matches without losing your assigned
      bumper.
- [ ] Flag one row you do not trust and say why.
