## Description

Power tools are faster. Hand tools are how you avoid needing a do-over
when the power tool was the wrong choice. A hex key that is fully seated
will not strip a #10-32. A hacksaw in a vise will cut a short leftover
when the bandsaw table is full. A file will take a burr off before it
takes a chunk out of someone's hand. This is the first veteran ticket:
fluency with the tools you should reach for *before* the drill.

[Shop Safety & PPE](../shop-safety/) must already be signed off. You will
use a hacksaw and a vise. You will not use the bandsaw, chop saw, drill
press, or grinder. Gloves stay off if a blade or a spinning bit is in
your hands; they can go on when you carry the cut end.

You already know from [Fasteners & Hardware](../fasteners-hardware/)
that a #10-32 SHCS wants a **5/32 in** hex and a button head usually
wants **1/8 in**. The failure mode is always the same: a key that is too
small, not seated, or levered at an angle. The socket rounds, the screw
is now a problem, and someone will spend a match break extracting it.
**L-keys** give you reach and, on the long arm, real leverage. **Ball
ends** exist so you can start a screw at an angle. They are not for
final torque — the ball is a smaller contact patch and will cam out.
Put the straight end in for the last turn.

Screwdrivers: match **type and size**. A #2 Phillips in a #1 head is a
strip waiting to happen. Torx and Robertson show up on some COTS
electronics; use those bits, not "close enough." Wrenches and sockets:
pull toward you when you can, so a slip does not punch you into the
robot. The right size does not round the hex. A 3/8 in nut is not a
10 mm nut, even if it feels close.

**Hacksaw:** the blade cuts on the **push** stroke. Teeth point forward
(away from the handle). A blade mounted backwards feels like you are
sawing with a butter knife and you will blame the aluminum. Use long
strokes, the full useful length of the blade, and let the teeth cut.
Short choppy strokes glaze the teeth. Clamp the work in a vise with the
cut line close to the jaws so the tube does not chatter.

**Files** also cut on the push. Lift on the return so you do not dull
the teeth riding backwards. A mill file is for flats; a round or
half-round is for inside curves. A **deburring tool** (the hooked one)
takes the knife-edge off a freshly cut tube in two seconds. Every cut
end gets deburred. That is not optional cosmetics — a burr will sit a
gusset off-square and it will cut the next person who grabs the part.

**Clamps and a vise** exist so both of your hands are on the tool, not
holding the work. Never hold a short leftover in your fingers and saw.
A **center punch** turns a layout cross from
[Measurement & Layout](../measurement-layout/) into a dimple the drill
can find. You may punch on this ticket if a mentor watches; you drill
in a later ticket.

Bring the marked scrap from measurement if you still have it. You will
cut *to a line* here with a hacksaw so that
[Cutting Materials](../cutting-materials/) can demand a machine cut of
the same quality.

## What you'll learn

- How to pick and seat the correct hex key, screwdriver, or wrench.
- How to clamp work and cut square with a hacksaw.
- How to file and deburr so a cut end is safe and a gusset sits flat.

## Tasks

1. **Watch the sawing motion before you make it.** Read
   [Popular Mechanics: How to Use a Hacksaw](https://www.popularmechanics.com/home/a42461426/how-to-use-a-hacksaw/)
   (or your shop's equivalent one-pager if a mentor prefers). Confirm
   tooth direction on a real blade: teeth point forward. If the shop
   blade is backwards, fix it with a mentor — do not "just start."

2. **Build a hex-key habit.** From the fastener wall, pick a #10-32
   SHCS and a 1/4-20 SHCS. Identify the two keys without looking at
   this page. Drive each into a nylock or a tapped scrap: seat fully,
   square, straight end for the last turn. Then start one screw with
   a **ball end** at an angle and finish with the straight end. Tell
   a mentor why you switched.

3. **Cut scrap square by hand.** Clamp a piece of 1×1 tube (or the
   leftover from your measurement piece) in a vise. Mark a square line
   with a combination square. Cut on the waste side with a hacksaw.
   Goals:

   - the cut stays on the waste side of the line
   - the end is close enough to square that a combination square does
     not show daylight you could slide a #10 shank through
   - you used long strokes and did not hold the tube in your fingers

4. **Deburr and file.** Deburr the inside and outside of the cut. File
   the end flat enough that a gusset or a square sits down without
   rocking. Run a finger *gently* along the edge only after you have
   looked — if it is still sharp, file again. Write your name on the
   piece. This is now a hand-cut standard you will compare to a machine
   cut later.

5. **Clamp something the unsafe way, then the safe way.** A mentor
   shows a short leftover. You explain why finger-holding it to saw or
   drill is how people get a spinning part or a cut hand. Then you
   clamp that leftover so both hands are free. No drilling yet.

## Acceptance Criteria

- [ ] You named the correct hex keys for a #10-32 SHCS and a 1/4-20
      SHCS and drove each without rounding the socket. You used a ball
      end only to start, then finished on the straight end.
- [ ] A hacksaw blade in the shop is confirmed teeth-forward. You said
      so to a mentor.
- [ ] A named piece of tube has a hacksaw cut on the waste side of a
      squared line, is deburred inside and out, and sits reasonably
      flat against a square. A mentor looked at it.
- [ ] You clamped a short leftover instead of holding it, and you
      explained why finger-holding is banned.

## Notes

- A dull hacksaw blade smears aluminum instead of cutting it. Ask for
  a new blade rather than leaning on a dead one.
- Files clog with aluminum. A file card (or a careful brush) cleans
  them. Chalk helps. Do not keep pushing a loaded file.
- You still do not run the bandsaw. That is the next machine-heavy
  stretch, starting after
  [Drills & Drivers](../drills-drivers/) teaches the cordless tools
  you will use every meeting.
