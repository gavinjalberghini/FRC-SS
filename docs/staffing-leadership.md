When a student is given a leadership role, they get three things at once: a
chance to exercise subject-matter expertise, a chance to represent fellow
students, and ownership of a process bigger than any one person. How you
introduce those leaders is a choice. None of the four patterns below is more
correct for an FRC team. They teach different things. This playbook implements
the third.

## Four patterns

| Pattern | Who holds the chairs | What decisions are based on | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Representative** | Students elected by the team | The consensus of the people they represent | Voice, fairness, advocacy |
| **Technical** | One student per trade, chosen by interview | Subject-matter knowledge | Ownership, standards, expertise |
| **Technical + adult gap-fill** | Student leads where ready; adults occupy empty chairs | Expertise first, with adult continuity | Ownership without forcing unready students |
| **Adult-centric** | Mentors hold the leads | Engineering judgment of working adults | Apprenticeship and industry connection |

### 1. Representative leadership

Students vote for a small set of leaders — three or five — who decide on
behalf of the people they represent. Those leaders extract opinions, bring
them to an open forum, and decide from the combined consensus of their
constituency.

This is the most individually fair method. Everyone is represented. You chose
the person who carries your voice into the room. It is also the weakest as a
robot team: popularity is not competency, and a parliament still has to invent
technical authority after the vote.

### 2. Technical leadership

Each competency area — the same trades as the
[Learning Hub]({{ '/learning/' | relative_url }}) tiles — has one lead.
Students interview for the role as they would for a job. Adults run the
interviews and make the selection. If you want student voice in the hiring,
put students on the panel and weigh their notes when you decide.

This puts decision-making power with the adults, and it gives the role a
written set of expectations. A student who is not selected can be told why.
When that leadership team meets, they decide from competing technical
judgment, not from a constituency they were elected to please.

### 3. Technical chairs, adults fill the gaps

This is the same as technical leadership, with one added rule: **when no
student can fulfill a chair, an adult occupies that named chair until a
student can take it.**

That rule does three things. It keeps a student from being put in a seat they
did not want or are not ready for. It gives adults a real hand in information
gathering and expertise without making every meeting adult-run. And it
preserves continuity between seasons — the chair still exists, even when the
senior class graduated.

The line that keeps this from collapsing into the fourth pattern: **adults
occupy a named chair. They do not hover over every chair.**

### 4. Adult-centric leads

Adults hold the leadership roles. There are honest reasons to do this. Working
engineers usually out-engineer high schoolers. The learning goal shifts from
project-management and technical leadership to a deeper apprenticeship —
mentorship from someone who does this for a living, and a clearer path to
industry connections.

That is a different challenge, not a lesser one. Treat it as a *phase* (a
rookie year, a safety-critical gap, a season with no seniors in a trade), not
as the team's philosophy, or it will fight the mission's "student-led" line.

## What this playbook implements

**Pattern 3.** One lead per trade, chosen by interview. Adults decide who sits
in the chair. Students may sit on the interview panel. If no student is ready,
an adult occupies that chair until one is. What each tier *does* day to day —
core path, Veteran, Lead — is on the
[Org Chart]({{ '/org-chart/' | relative_url }}).

This is the most reasonable way to keep continuity between seasons, let
students exercise real ownership, and shield students from seats they do not
want or are not ready for.

It also resolves a tension elsewhere in this playbook. The
[mission]({{ '/mission/' | relative_url }}) says the team is student-led, with
mentors coaching rather than directing. The
[Decision-Making Guide]({{ '/decision-making/' | relative_url }}) says adults
have the final say. Both are true under this pattern: students own the chairs
they are ready for; adults fill empty ones and hold veto.

## How a lead is chosen

- The chair is a **job**, not a popularity contest. Candidates interview against
  a written description of the role — expectations, time, and what "good"
  looks like.
- **Adults make the selection.** Students may interview alongside them; their
  notes count. The final call stays with the adults so there is a point of
  enforcement for the requirements.
- Eligibility is earned through the [Learning Hub]({{ '/learning/' | relative_url }})
  — you cannot interview for Lead in a trade you have not demonstrated.
- A student who is not selected gets a clear reason. That is a feature of the
  interview, not an awkward leftover.

## How a gap is filled

- If no student can sit in a chair, an **adult occupies that named chair** —
  same responsibilities, same seat at the leadership table.
- The adult's job is to keep the function alive *and* to grow a student who
  can take it. The handoff is the success condition, not an afterthought.
- Do not leave an empty chair. Combining two trades is only for seasons when
  that chair should not exist, not as a way to avoid the adult sitting in it.
