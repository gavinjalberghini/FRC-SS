[How We Staff Leadership]({{ '/staffing-leadership/' | relative_url }})
decides who sits in a chair. The
[Org Chart]({{ '/org-chart/' | relative_url }}) decides the tiers. This
page is the job.

A Lead who attends the leadership meeting and then goes quiet has the
title. The work is what the trade can still do when that person is at a
game, and what the trade can still do in September after they have a
college ID.

An adult who occupies an empty chair owes the same job. Sitting in the
seat means doing it. Growing a student who can take the chair is still
the success condition.

The Student Leadership Team's shared work stays what the org chart
already names: **facilitate the success of others**, **shape the work**
to this year's roster, and **help the adults decide**. The duties below
are how a co-captain carries that job between meetings.

## Make the trade's communication available

Information does not walk itself to the leadership table. On a night
when six people are working electrical, the electrical Lead reaches
those six — the veteran on the power distribution hub and the freshman
on ferrules — and leaves with a picture: what moved, what is blocked,
who is waiting on a part, who is stuck and quiet.

That picture has to be readable by people who were not in that corner
of the shop. Put it in the team's standard place. If the team
coordinates in Slack, the update is a Slack post. If the team tracks
work as tickets, the update lives on the ticket. Pick one place and
keep using it. A hallway recap that ends when the Lead drives home
leaves the rest of the team guessing.

You still own the update when you are not in the building. A test, a
game, a sick day: follow up with the **students who were there** and
write what they tell you. Ask the adult who was in the room only when
those students cannot give you the picture. "I wasn't there" is not a
status report, and neither is "the mentor will fill you in" if you have
not yet asked the students.

What that looks like:

- One written update for the trade, the same day or the next morning.
  Done, next, blocked. Names and parts, not a vibe.
- You talked to the people doing the work, including the quiet ones,
  not only the veteran who already texts you.
- If you were out, you asked the students who were in before you asked
  the adult.
- Another Lead, or a mentor who was across the shop, can read the
  trade's state without calling you.

Veterans still bring blockers to their Lead. That duty is on the org
chart. The Lead still goes and gets the picture when they do not.

[Communication Fundamentals]({{ '/learning/leadership/communication-fundamentals/' | relative_url }})
and
[Running Effective Meetings]({{ '/learning/leadership/effective-meetings/' | relative_url }})
teach the skill. This page is the duty: the update exists even when you
were not in the room.

## Build the trade past this season

A student-led team has to be able to build again next September, after
this year's seniors are gone. Graduation is on a calendar. The wiring
map, the standup habit, and how a new member is treated have to outlive
the person who currently holds them.

The Lead trains new members in person. Standing at the crimper with
someone who is new is how you learn whether the training is real. Two
more jobs sit on the same duty:

- **Review and write the training.** Learning Hub tickets, a one-page
  standard, a pit card, a checklist a Veteran can run without you.
  Knowledge that lives only in a senior's memory leaves with them in
  May.
- **Put Veterans on that same work.** Veterans already owe mentorship.
  You assign it, you check it, and "I will just do it, it is faster"
  does not get to be the season plan. A Veteran who only builds is a
  single point of failure the chair chose to keep.

Name who could hold the chair next year while you can still coach them.
Skip the coronation in the group chat. Give them a stretch this season:
a meeting they run, a standard they teach, a ticket they revise with
you. The
[Mentorship & Succession]({{ '/learning/leadership/mentorship-succession/' | relative_url }})
ticket is how you practice it. The
[mission]({{ '/mission/' | relative_url }}) already states the point —
invest in teammates today so the team is stronger after any one of you
has moved on.

## Be the bridge — and the first follower

The chair sits between students and adults. Traffic goes both ways.

**Students to adults.** A freshman who is lost, a safety worry, a "this
call feels unfair" should be able to reach an adult through the Lead.
Carry the concern in the student's words. If the only news you take
upstairs is good news, adults are deciding on a filtered shop. You
should be willing to say the awkward thing in the room.

**Adults to students.** When adults put a requirement or a suggestion
on the table — glasses before the mill, a code freeze on Friday, a new
start time, a constraint you did not pick — the Lead practices
**first-follower** behavior.

Derek Sivers' [lesson from a dancing guy](https://sive.rs/ff) (the
[three-minute talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/derek_sivers_how_to_start_a_movement))
is the picture. Someone starts something that looks odd alone. One
person joins in public, and new people copy that follower. The first
follower is a form of leadership: it is what turns a solo act into
something a group will do. The same idea, applied to teams, is in
[Why the first follower is the key to innovation](https://medium.com/innovate-624/why-the-first-follower-is-the-key-to-innovation-a424718eed00)
and
[The power of the first follower](https://loginstitute.ca/blogs/leadership-hub/leadership-the-power-of-the-first-follower).

In this shop the new move is often an adult requirement the students
have not taken up yet. The room watches the Lead. When the electrical
Lead puts the glasses on and says "we are doing this — here is how on
our side," the trade has a person to copy. When the Lead shrugs in the
group chat, the requirement stays a speech. Show the trade how to
follow, where they can see you, and make it about the work.

Bring a real objection before the call: unsafe, illegal, or impossible
goes to the adults while the decision is still open. After the call,
follow. The
[contract]({{ '/contract/' | relative_url }}) already asks every member
to support a decision they argued against. The Lead goes first, in
public.

## Thirteen behaviors of a high-trust leader

Those duties stall when the room does not trust the person in the
chair. People discount the update, hide the bad news, and wait to see
if the requirement is real. Stephen M.R. Covey, in *The Speed of
Trust*, treats trust as **character plus competence** and names thirteen
behaviors high-trust leaders practice. Character without competence is
a kind person who cannot close a harness. Competence without character
is a senior the new members stop asking.

The names, and the character / competence split, are Covey's. A
walkthrough is on
[FranklinCovey's note on the thirteen behaviours](https://www.franklincovey.co.uk/blog/2021/10/11/the-13-behaviours-of-high-trust/).
The shop column is this playbook's.

### Character

| Behavior | In this shop |
| --- | --- |
| **Talk Straight** | Call a late harness late. Say what you mean in the update and in the room. Leave the spin out of Slack. |
| **Demonstrate Respect** | The freshman on zip ties is a person whose time counts. Fierce about the robot, kind about the human — Gracious Professionalism® with a name. |
| **Create Transparency** | The trade's state is somewhere another Lead can open. "Ask me if you want to know" is a closed door. |
| **Right Wrongs** | When you drop a commitment or a crimp, say so and repair it. A quiet redo that nobody learns from leaves the same mistake for next week. |
| **Show Loyalty** | Give the credit in the room. Talk about a teammate who is absent the way you would if they were at the table. |

### Competence

| Behavior | In this shop |
| --- | --- |
| **Deliver Results** | The update gets written. The training happens. The part you said would be ordered gets ordered. A chair that only attends meetings loses the trade's confidence. |
| **Get Better** | Improve the checklist, the ticket, and your own habit. Ask what the trade should do differently next week, even when your hands are already the fastest in the room. |
| **Confront Reality** | Name the thing the room is stepping around: the intake will not be ready, the grade is slipping, the design breaks the constraint. Hope is not a status. |
| **Clarify Expectations** | Owner, outcome, and time. "Work on electrical" is how Thursday becomes a surprise. When the plan changes, say the new expectation out loud. |
| **Practice Accountability** | Start with yourself, then the trade — including the Veteran you asked to teach someone. "They didn't show up" still belongs to the person who was supposed to follow up. |

### Character and competence

| Behavior | In this shop |
| --- | --- |
| **Listen First** | Hear the student before you carry their concern upstairs. Hear the adult before you translate a requirement downstairs. |
| **Keep Commitments** | Say what you will do, then do it — including the update from a meeting you missed. Make fewer promises when you cannot keep them. |
| **Extend Trust** | Hand Veterans real training and real tasks, with the authority to finish them. Redoing every crimp after you said you trusted them is hovering. |

You will not hit all thirteen on a Thursday. Notice the counterfeit
versions: a vague assignment, credit you kept, "I trust you" followed
by taking the tool back. The
[leadership tickets]({{ '/learning/leadership/' | relative_url }})
practice these one habit at a time. The chair is held to the list.
